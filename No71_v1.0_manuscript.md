## Definitions

For n >= 1, let V_n be all subsets S of [n] that do not contain any of the consecutive triples {i,i+1,i+2}, 1 <= i <= n-2. Equivalently, these are all length-n binary words avoiding 111. In Q_n(111), two vertices are adjacent when their supports differ by exactly one element. Since V_n is downward closed, deleting a 1-bit always gives a legal vertex.

A 1-perfect code is a subset C of V_n such that each vertex has **exactly one** codeword in its closed neighborhood (itself or a one-bit flip). Distinct codeword closed neighborhoods do not overlap.

## Position relative to earlier work

The graph $Q_n(111)$ is the generalized Fibonacci cube denoted $\Gamma_n(111)=\Gamma_n(1^3)$ in the cited literature. It is distinct from the ordinary Fibonacci cube $\Gamma_n(11)$ and from the cyclic Lucas cube.

Mollard (2018) studied perfect codes in generalized Fibonacci cubes\(\Gamma_n(1^s)\), namely binary words avoiding a run of s ones. His Conjecture 3.1 states that, for n>=3 and s>=1, **if** a perfect code C exists in \(\Gamma_n(1^s)\), then n=2^p-1 for some integer p **and furthermore C is a perfect code in the full n-cube Q_n**. The present fixed-s=3 classification, **if the proof survives further independent verification**, establishes this conjecture for s=3: the only n>=3 with a perfect code is n=3, and the exhibited C={100,011} is also perfect in Q_3. The main theorem also handles n=1 outside Mollard's n>=3 hypothesis.

Mollard's existence corollary covers n=2^p-1 for s>=3*2^(p-2), giving s=3 for p=2 (n=3) but not for p>=3. His discussion gives a code for n=7, s=5; this does not decide n=7, s=3. The 2018 paper consequently does not subsume the fixed-s=3 nonexistence statement here. This is a **scope comparison, not a comprehensive novelty or priority determination**.

## Main theorem

**For every integer n >= 1, Q_n(111) admits a 1-perfect code if and only if n=1 or n=3.** For n=1, use C={0}. For n=3, use C={100,011}; their closed neighborhoods partition exactly the seven legal words. We prove nonexistence otherwise.

### Lemma 1. For n>=4, the empty set is not a codeword.

Suppose it were. Then all singleton vertices are dominated by the empty set, so none of them is a codeword. Nor can a pair {a,b} be a codeword: the singleton {a} would then have two dominating centers, the empty word and {a,b}. Every pair of [n] must therefore be dominated by a triple codeword containing it. Every triple in V_n is legal only if it is not a consecutive triple; it nevertheless has all three pairs as legal lower neighbors. The triple codewords form a Steiner triple system on the n coordinates. If n is not congruent to 1 or 3 modulo 6, a Steiner triple system cannot exist, giving the required contradiction immediately. It remains to consider n congruent to 1 or 3 modulo 6, when the system would have n(n-1)/6 triple codewords.

Exactly n-2 of all binom(n,3) triples are forbidden, so N_3 = binom(n,3)-(n-2). Every legal triple not itself a codeword must be dominated by a four-element codeword: there are no pair codewords, and a center of equal weight cannot be adjacent to a different triple. Each legal four-element codeword dominates exactly four legal triples by deletion of one coordinate. Thus

  4 | N_3 - n(n-1)/6 = n(n-1)(n-3)/6 - (n-2).

For n=1 or 3 modulo 6, the first term on the right is even, while n-2 is odd. The right side is odd, contradiction. Hence the empty word is not a codeword. QED.

### Lemma 2. For n>=4, a code would have odd n, one singleton center e_j, and a matching M of pair codewords on [n]\\{j}.

By Lemma 1, the empty vertex has to be dominated by precisely one singleton center e_j. Every other singleton e_a (a != j) cannot be a center and must be dominated by a pair codeword {a,b}. (No pair containing j can be a center because it is adjacent to e_j.) Each pair codeword dominates exactly two such singleton vertices, and each of the n-1 remaining singleton vertices has exactly one dominating pair. Therefore these pair codewords form a perfect matching M of [n]\\{j}, and n-1 is even. Thus n is odd. QED.

### Lemma 3 (endpoint rigidity). For n>=5, j is 1 or n. If j=1, {2,3} is an edge of M.

For every forbidden consecutive triple containing j, delete j and retain the other two indices. These pairs form an edge set F_j on X=[n]\\{j}. A direct inspection of consecutive triples shows that F_j is a path of length 1 at an endpoint j=1 or j=n, length 2 at j=2 or j=n-1, and length 3 at all remaining interior j. In particular, F_j is a forest, and for an interior j it contains two adjacent edges.

Fix a in X. Consider legal three-element vertices {j,a,b} with b in X\\{a} that are not dominated by the unique pair codeword of M incident to a. There are exactly

  n-3-deg_(F_j\\M)(a)

such vertices: of the n-2 choices of b, delete the forbidden choices (edges of F_j incident with a), then delete the matching partner unless it was already forbidden. None of these triples can be a codeword (any codeword containing j and two other elements shares a closed-neighborhood vertex with e_j). No other pair codeword can dominate them. Hence they are dominated by four-element codewords containing {j,a}. Every such four-element center dominates exactly *two* triples of this family, by deleting each of its other two elements. Downward closure makes both triples legal; neither can be dominated by the matching pair center as well, since that would violate unique domination. Thus both are among the triples being counted. Therefore n-3-deg_(F_j\\M)(a) is even.

As n is odd, deg_(F_j\\M)(a) is even for every a. A nonempty forest contains an odd-degree vertex. Hence F_j\\M must be empty and F_j is contained in M. If j were interior, F_j would contain adjacent edges, impossible in a matching. Therefore j is an endpoint. In the j=1 case, F_1={{2,3}}, so {2,3} is in M. QED.

### Lemma 4 (odd fan obstruction). For every odd n>=5, the endpoint configuration of Lemma 3 is impossible.

By reversal, suppose j=1. Lemma 3 forces the singleton center {1} and pair center {2,3}. Let P={2,n} and S={1,2,n}. Both vertices are legal; P is not a pair codeword because 2 is already matched to 3. As no singleton center dominates P, its unique dominating center is a triple R=P union {c} for some c in L={4,5,...,n-1}. The index c cannot be 3, since {2,3} is already a codeword and would be adjacent to {2,3,n}.

The legal triple S contains the singleton-center coordinate 1, so S cannot be a codeword: for instance the vertex {1,2} would lie in the closed neighborhoods of both S and the singleton codeword {1}. More generally, no triple containing 1 can be a codeword, since deleting either of its other coordinates produces a legal pair adjacent to both that triple and {1}. It is not dominated by a pair center: none of its pairs is a codeword (the two pairs containing 1 overlap the singleton center, and P is not in M). Hence its unique dominating center is a four-element word D=S union {d}, with d in L. The index 3 is not allowed, since S union {3} contains 111 at positions 1,2,3. The centers R and D must be distinct and have disjoint closed neighborhoods. In particular c != d, because if c=d, R and D are adjacent. **For n=5, the set L={4} has only one element, so distinct c,d in L cannot exist: this already gives a contradiction.** Hence below it suffices to consider odd n>=7.

Define the family of legal four-element vertices

  W_x=S union {x}, for x in L={4,...,n-1}.

There are |L|=n-4 of these. Exactly one is dominated by a codeword of weight 3: any triple codeword dominating W_x must be a three-element subset, and as no triple codeword contains 1 (by the singleton {1}), its support must be {2,n,x}. Since P has exactly one dominating triple R, this occurs exactly when x=c. Exactly one W_x is a codeword of weight 4: any such W_x would also dominate S, which has unique dominating codeword D, so W_x=D, occurring exactly when x=d. Weight-2 or smaller centers cannot dominate weight-4 words, and a distinct weight-4 center cannot be adjacent to a weight-4 word in a binary cube (equal-weight words differ in an even number of bits).

Every remaining W_x must thus be dominated by a five-element center. Such a center is necessarily S union {x,z}, with z in L distinct from x: all legal centers contain S, the bit z=3 is forbidden, and the other positions in S are already present. A five-element center of this form dominates exactly two vertices of the family, namely W_x and W_z. As the closed neighborhoods of codewords partition the vertices, no such five-element center can also dominate either of the two already covered words W_c and W_d. Consequently, for odd n>=7, the n-6 other words must be partitioned into pairs. Thus n-6 is even, implying n even, contrary to Lemma 2. Combined with the preceding n=5 contradiction, this completes the lemma. QED.

### Completion

For even n>=4, Lemma 2 gives a contradiction. For odd n>=5, Lemmas 2--4 give a contradiction. At n=2, the graph is Q_2, a 4-cycle whose closed balls each have size 3, so a perfect partition is impossible. Finally, the explicit codes at n=1 and n=3 give existence. Therefore the theorem holds for every n>=1. QED.
